\begin{abstract}
We solve time-harmonic wave scattering problems on unbounded domains without domain truncation by mapping the unbounded domain to a bounded domain and scaling the oscillatory decay towards infinity. The technique, first developed in numerical relativity for time-domain wave equations, solves for the far-field pattern using compactification at infinity, avoiding the outer boundary problem. We design a layer that restricts the transformations to an annular domain. The resulting \emph{Null Infinity Layer} solves both the outer boundary and radiation extraction problems. We demonstrate its practical implementation using finite difference, Chebyshev collocation, spectral-Galerkin, and finite element methods in one and two dimensions. 
\end{abstract}

\subsection{The main problem}
Wave propagation problems on unbounded domains appear in many areas of science and technology. The equations that describe these problems vary widely in complexity and structure: scalar wave equations describe acoustic and seismic waves, vector wave equations describe electromagnetic waves, and tensor wave equations describe gravitational waves. The equations may have variable coefficients or include nonlinear terms depending on the application. Despite this diversity, solutions to wave equations share some essential properties.

Wave equations have oscillatory solutions propagating to infinity, meaning generic solutions decay slowly. When the energy of a propagating wave is conserved, the amplitude of the wave in $d$-dimensional space decays only as $r^{\frac{1-d}{2}}$ where $r$ is the distance to the source of the wave. 

This slow decay makes waves important in applications because we can measure them far from the source. The computation of the far-field pattern is the primary goal in many problems. The echo area for acoustic waves, the radar cross-section for electromagnetic waves, or the gravitational flux for gravitational waves are all defined via a limit to infinite distance. The numerical computation of solutions to wave equations at infinity is a difficult problem. The essential difficulty is computing oscillatory solutions in unbounded domains. Consider a time-harmonic wave, $e^{\iu k x}$, with wavenumber $k$, filling out the unbounded spatial domain, $x\in(-\infty,\infty)$. We consider the following question: \emph{How can we solve an equation describing infinitely many oscillations with the finite resources of a computer?}

This paper answers the above question by employing relativistic methods, particularly the unification of space and time into spacetime, hyperbolic geometry, and conformal structure. The resulting method allows us to compute oscillatory solutions to time-harmonic wave equations on unbounded domains without resorting to approximations beyond numerical discretization. The methods are agnostic of the fundamental solution and can therefore be applied to various wave propagation problems in science and engineering.

\subsection{Helmholtz equation}
One of the simplest cases where the computational difficulties with wave equations are apparent is the linear, constant-coefficient, source-free Helmholtz equation. Consider the problem of time-harmonic wave scattering from a bounded obstacle $\Gamma \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ in $d$ dimensions. The scattered wave, $U$, satisfies the Helmholtz equation and the Sommerfeld radiation condition at infinity,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:helm}
	\begin{gathered}
		\Delta U + k^2 U = F \quad \mathrm{in } \ \mathbb{R}^d\setminus \Gamma, \\
		\partial_r U - \iu k U = o\left(r^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\right) \quad \mathrm{as} \ r\to\infty,\ d=1,2,3.
	\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Dirichlet or Neumann conditions are given on the surface of the scatterer $\Gamma$. The two computational difficulties for this problem are: (i) the unbounded domain and (ii) the highly oscillatory behavior of the solution for high wavenumbers $k$. Both difficulties have been active areas of research with an extensive list of proposed treatments (see reviews \cite{TSYNKOV1998465, HagstromReview}).

% Past effort
A wide range of techniques have been developed to treat unbounded domains for wave equations \cite{givoli2013numerical}. Among these, domain-truncation methods are popular for their convenience and generality. Representative examples include high-order absorbing boundary conditions (ABCs) \cite{EngquistMajda77, BaylissTurkel80} and absorbing regions such as perfectly matched layers (PML) \cite{israeli1981approximation, BERENGER1994185}. PML damps outgoing waves before they meet the outer boundary, substantially reducing spurious reflections. Among ABCs, spectrally convergent constructions are available and often sufficiently accurate in practice \cite{hagstrom2009complete}.

% Global methods such as the boundary integral method do provide the unbounded domain solution and are the preferred method for the constant-coefficient Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:helm}. However, they rely on the availability of the fundamental solution (the Green function) and do not generalize to other equations where the Green function is not available in closed form.

In certain cases, one can enforce the radiation condition exactly at the artificial boundary. Classical Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) or non-reflecting boundary conditions are exact but typically nonlocal (in space and/or time) and can be expensive to evaluate \cite{keller1989exact, GROTE1998327, grote1996nonreflecting}. Fast algorithms for the associated convolution kernels mitigate this cost \cite{alpert2000rapid}. 

Infinite element methods \cite{demkowicz2006few, halla2016convergence, nannen2022complex} encode the outgoing behavior into special shape functions. Global boundary-integral equations (BIE) \cite{chandler2012numerical, klockner2013quadrature} compute the unbounded-domain solution by reformulating the PDE as integral equations on the boundary. For the constant-coefficient Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:helm} this approach is often preferred, as it builds in the radiation condition and provides far-field quantities essentially at no extra cost. Its applicability, however, relies on access to the fundamental solution and becomes less straightforward for variable-coefficient or time-dependent problems.

% Caveats of past effort
In short, there exist excellent methods that are exact under their modeling assumptions (e.g., BIE, DtN, non-reflecting conditions, infinite elements, and exact PML), as well as highly effective approximate local methods (e.g., high-order ABCs, absorbing layers) \cite{givoli2013numerical, 9196168, kirby2020finite, papadimitropoulos2020double, petropavlovsky2020numerical}. What remains challenging is a treatment that is simultaneously (i) local to standard interior discretizations, (ii) exact across a broad class of geometries and media, and (iii) simple to implement and robust in practice. Moreover, while BIE and infinite element methods yield far-field data directly, domain truncation methods require a separate post-processing step for far-field evaluation.

In principle, mapping the unbounded domain to a bounded one would carry the global problem into a finite computational domain. However, spatial coordinate mappings applied to oscillatory solutions are known to compress the wavelength near the mapped boundary, which cannot be resolved and produces reflections unless the grid is refined without bound \cite{GroschOrszag77, boyd1982optimization, shen2014approximations}. The mapping translates the infinite domain problem to an infinite resolution problem and the difficulties remain.

\subsection{Proposed solution}
We solve the infinite resolution problem by suitably transforming the time coordinate (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:tau} and \cite{Zenginoglu08, ZENGINOGLU20112286}). The time transformation of the wave equation translates to a rescaling in the frequency domain for the Helmholtz equation, which removes the asymptotic oscillations (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:rescaling} and \cite{ZengFramework}). As a result, an infinite domain with infinite oscillations is mapped to a finite domain with a finite number of oscillations (see Fig.~\ref{fig:flattening}).

This approach is inspired by Penrose compactification and the concept of conformal infinity in relativity \cite{Penrose, Penrose65, Frauendiener2004, Winicour2012, kroon2017conformal}. It consists of three steps: (i) scaling out the asymptotic radial fall-off, (ii) scaling out asymptotic radial oscillations, and (iii) mapping the unbounded domain to a bounded one. This method is an application of hyperboloidal compactification (\cite{Zenginoglu08, ZENGINOGLU20112286, Macedo_2020, panosso2024hyperboloidal}) to time-harmonic solutions \cite{ZengFramework, ansorg2016spectral, macedo2018hyperboloidal, bizon2020toy, jaramillo2021pseudospectrum}. The outer boundary of the mapped domain corresponds to \emph{null infinity}, which refers to infinity along characteristic (null) directions (see \SM \ref{sec:app_penrose} for details). We refer to this method as \emph{null infinity compactification} (NIC). Intuitively, null infinity can be thought of as the ideal boundary of the asymptotically hyperbolic manifold arising from the time transformation.

This paper demonstrates the application of NIC to solve the Helmholtz equation on unbounded domains using local methods. Because NIC provides access to the entire solution, it eliminates the need for approximate boundary conditions, introducing only discretization errors. Moreover, NIC gives direct access to the solution at infinity, simplifying the extraction of radiative solutions in the far field. This feature is crucial in many applications where observables are defined at infinity, such as the echo area in acoustics, the radar cross-section in electromagnetics, and the gravitational waveform in general relativity.

Steps (i) and (ii) transform solutions to the Helmholtz equation from algebraic decay with oscillations towards infinity to no decay without oscillations towards infinity. Geometrically, step (i) corresponds to a conformal rescaling, while step (ii) represents a time transformation in the frequency domain \cite{ZengFramework}. 
% This separation of the unknown into oscillatory and non-oscillatory components resembles the geometrical optics decomposition into amplitude and phase \cite{engquist2003computational}, except here the phase is a prescribed function.

An advantage of NIC in the frequency domain over the time domain is the additional freedom one has in choosing the time transformation. In the time domain, the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition limits the transformation, whereas in the frequency domain, no such restriction applies. This flexibility allows us to choose a characteristic time transformation, as demonstrated in Sec.~\ref{sec:numerical}.

Restricting the transformations to an annular domain gives us a layer representing the infinite exterior domain with the boundary at null infinity. This null infinity layer (NIL) differs from the hyperboloidal layer of \cite{ZENGINOGLU20112286} because it can use both characteristic and hyperboloidal coordinates. NIL with characteristic coordinates is similar to the perfectly absorbing layer of \cite{wang2017perfect, yang2021truly} except that the solution is not artificially damped in the layer, and the radiative solution at infinity can be obtained at the numerical outer boundary. In addition, NIL does not rely on the local Green function. The method can be applied to equations with variable coefficients or nonlinear source terms if certain asymptotic fall-off conditions are satisfied. 

The paper is structured as follows. In Sect.~\ref{sec:nic1d}, we introduce NIC using a simple one-dimensional example and explain the underlying regularization mechanism. Sect.~\ref{sec:examples} presents specific examples for NIC. Sect.~\ref{sec:nic_general} extends the method to higher dimensions and variable coefficients.
% and presents an argument for inverse scattering problems. 
Sect.~\ref{sec:nil} demonstrates how the transformations can be confined to a layer of finite thickness and relates NIL to common layer methods. Finally, Sect.~\ref{sec:numerical} provides numerical experiments in one- and two dimensions using finite difference, Chebyshev collocation, spectral-Galerkin, and finite element methods.

\section{Null infinity compactification in one dimension}\label{sec:nic1d}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Spatial compactification for equations with oscillatory solutions is ineffective because infinitely many oscillations cannot be resolved on a finite, compactified domain \cite{GroschOrszag77, shen2014approximations}. As an explicit and representative example, consider the one-dimensional plane wave\footnote{Similar arguments apply for the one-dimensional Green's function proportional to $e^{\iu k |x-x'|}$.}, $U_{s+}(r) = e^{\iu k r}$. Compactification with $\rho=\tanh r$ from the unbounded domain $r\in[0,\infty)$ to the bounded domain $\rho\in[0,1)$ gives
\[ U_{s+}(\rho) =   e^{\iu k \arctanh\rho} = \left(\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\right)^{\frac{\iu k}{2}}.\]
The solution is singular at the domain boundary, $\rho=1$. However, we can remove the singularity by a rescaling. The rescaled single-mode solution,
\be\label{eq:plane_tanh} u_{s+}(\rho) =  (1-\rho^2)^{\frac{\iu k}{2}} U_s(\rho) = (1+\rho)^{\iu k}, \ee
is a regular function on the bounded $\rho$-domain. Compactifying the bounded domain by adding the point at infinity, we can solve the transformed Helmholtz equation for the rescaled variable $u(\rho)$ on $\rho\in[0,1]$. 

This combination of rescaling and compactification is the central idea behind NIC. To demonstrate its effect on differential equations, consider the one-dimensional Helmholtz equation subject to the Sommerfeld radiation condition:
\begin{align}
	\label {eq:Helmholtz1d} d_r^2 U + k^2 U &= 0, \quad r\in[0,\infty),\\
	\label{eq:Sommerfeld1d}d_r U - \iu k U &= 0 \quad \mathrm{as} \ r\to\infty.
\end{align}
Given boundary conditions at $r=0$, the system above has a unique solution. The Sommerfeld condition \eqref{eq:Sommerfeld1d} is necessary to ensure uniqueness because the Helmholtz equation describes propagation for both incoming and outgoing waves. For example, we have two plane wave solutions satisfying $U(0) = 1$: an outgoing one, $U_{s+}(r) = e^{\iu k r}$, and an incoming one, $U_{s-}(r) = e^{-\iu k r}$. Without a selection criterion, the general solution is a linear combination of these two modes. The Sommerfeld condition \eqref{eq:Sommerfeld1d} ensures that only the outgoing wave remains. Next, we discuss the general method. 

\subsection{Rescaling}
We scale out asymptotic oscillations by
\be\label{eq:rescaling} U(r) = e^{\iu k h(r)} u(r).\ee
We refer to $h(r)$ as the height function as is common in relativity \cite{reinhart1973maximal, beig1996vacuum}. The reason for the name will be explained in Sec.~\ref{sec:tt} where we describe the connection of the rescaling \eqref{eq:rescaling} to time transformations. We  choose a height function that approaches $r$ in the asymptotic limit. This rescaling flattens the waves such that the effective wavenumber of rescaled solutions vanishes at infinity. The Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:Helmholtz1d} for the rescaled variable $u(r)$ in \eqref{eq:rescaling} reads
\be\label{eq:resc_helm}
d_r^2 u + 2 \iu k H \, d_r u + \left[ k^2 (1-H^2) + \iu k\, d_r H\right] u = 0.
\ee
The boost function, $H(r) :=d_r h(r)$, is the derivative of the height function and satisfies the following conditions \cite{Zenginoglu08, ZengFramework, jaramillo2021pseudospectrum}
\be \label{eq:h_cond}
	|H| \leq 1, \quad \lim_{r\to\infty} H = 1, \quad \lim_{r\to\infty} d_r H = 0.
\ee
The conditions ensure that the effective wavenumber, $k^2(1-H^2)$, vanishes asymptotically as $r\to\infty$, and the rescaling \eqref{eq:rescaling} corresponds to a time transformation, as discussed in the next section. For a more detailed recent discussion, see \cite{zenginouglu2024hyperbolic, zenginouglu2025bridging, panosso2024hyperboloidal}.


\subsection{Time transformation and asymptotic flattening of waves} \label{sec:tt}
Rescalings of the type \eqref{eq:rescaling} appear in various methods on Helmholtz equations with $h(r)=r$. This choice removes all oscillations from the outgoing plane wave solution and corresponds to compactification along characteristic (null) surfaces. The infinite element method \cite{demkowicz2006few, halla2016convergence, nannen2022complex}, boundary integral methods \cite{chandler2012numerical}, the exact PML \cite{hugonin2005perfectly, bermudez2008exact}, and the perfect absorbing layer \cite{wang2017perfect, yang2021truly, wang2025novel} all use a similar rescaling to remove oscillations from the outgoing solution. The pole condition in \cite{schmidt2008pole} is also related to this rescaling through the Laplace-transformation in combination with the scaling out of the asymptotic decay. While this idea is not new, recognizing its relationship to time transformations allows us to generalize these methods in a geometric framework. When the boost is strictly less than unity, we get compactification along hyperboloidal time surfaces. Such surfaces are asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds whose ideal boundary corresponds to infinity along characteristic directions (null infinity) \cite{friedrich1986, zenginouglu2024hyperbolic}.

To see how such rescalings correspond to time transformations, we recap the derivation of the Helmholtz equation from the time-domain wave equation. Consider the wave equation for the scalar function $V(r,t)$ in spacetime coordinates $(r,t)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)$ with unit speed of propagation
\be\label{eq:1dwave} \partial_t^2 V - \partial_r^2 V = 0\,. \ee
We make a time-harmonic ansatz with a single frequency, $k$,
\be\label{eq:ansatz} 
V(r,t)= e^{-\iu k t} \, U(r).
\ee 
With this ansatz, the partial time derivative in the wave equation \eqref{eq:1dwave} becomes a multiplication, $\partial_t\to-\iu k$, and the partial radial derivative becomes a total derivative, $\partial_r\to d_r$, which leads to the Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:Helmholtz1d} for $U(r)$.

We now introduce a new time coordinate, $\tau$, that satisfies $\partial_\tau=\partial_t$, so that the transformed equation has time-independent coefficients. In relativity, this requirement corresponds to the invariance of the time translation symmetry of the underlying Minkowski metric. The time transformation takes the form \cite{reinhart1973maximal, gowdy_wave_1981, beig1996vacuum, calabrese2006asymptotically, Zenginoglu08},
\be\label{eq:tau} \tau = t - h(r).\ee
The function $h(r)$ determines the height of the new time coordinate $\tau$ with respect to $t$ on a spacetime diagram as on the left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:flattening}. The conditions \eqref{eq:h_cond} ensure that level sets of $\tau$ remain spacelike or null. The single frequency ansatz in $\tau$ reads,
\[ V(r, \tau) = e^{-\iu k \tau} u(r) = e^{-\iu k t} e^{\iu k h(r)} u(r)\,.\]
Comparing with \eqref{eq:ansatz}, we see that the time transformation \eqref{eq:tau} in time domain corresponds to the rescaling \eqref{eq:rescaling} in frequency domain \cite{ZengFramework, ansorg2016spectral, macedo2018hyperboloidal, marchner2021stable}.

The asymptotic flattening of the waves due to the time transformation \eqref{eq:tau} is best understood in a time-domain solution. Consider the outgoing sine wave $V_s(r,t) = \sin(t-r)$. We introduce a time coordinate whose level sets are spacetime hyperbola,
\be\label{eq:hyperbola} \tau = t - \sqrt{1+r^2}.\ee
Time coordinates resembling spacetime hyperbola in their causal behavior are called hyperboloidal \cite{friedrich1986, zenginouglu2024hyperbolic}. Their two main properties are that their level sets allow the definition of a time coordinate, and that they approach the null cone asymptotically. The hyperboloidal sine wave becomes $V_s(r,\tau) = \sin\left(\tau + \sqrt{1+r^2} - r\right)$. We calculate the effective wavenumber by the phase derivative, $k_\mathrm{eff} :=| \partial_r \left(\tau + \sqrt{1+r^2} - r\right) | = 1-r/\sqrt{1+r^2}$. The vanishing of this function as $r\to\infty$ indicates the asymptotic flattening the waves as visualized in the spacetime diagram Fig.~\ref{fig:flattening}.

\subsection{Null infinity compactification}\label{sec:nic}
The rescaling \eqref{eq:rescaling} flattens outgoing waves and makes the Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:resc_helm} amenable to compactification. Such spatial mappings from unbounded to bounded domains are well-known and have been extensively studied in the context of wave equations \cite{GroschOrszag77, boyd1982optimization, shen2009some, wang2017perfect}. We write the mapping in a general form as
\be\label{eq:mapping} r = r(\rho), \quad r\in[0,\infty),\ \rho\in[0,S), \ee
such that
\begin{align}\label{eqn:G}
	 & \frac{d\rho}{dr} = \frac{1}{r'(\rho)} \equiv G(\rho) > 0, \quad \rho\in [0,S), \\
	 & G(S)      = 0, \qquad r(S) = \infty.\nonumber
\end{align}
Here, $S$ is the location of the outer boundary. The spatial derivative operator transforms under the mapping as
\[ d_r = \frac{d\rho}{dr} d_\rho = G(\rho) d_\rho.  \]
Equation \eqref{eq:resc_helm} becomes after a division by $G$,
\be\label{eq:trafo_helm}
d_\rho (G d_\rho u) + 2 \iu k H d_\rho u + \left(k^2 \frac{1-H^2}{G} + \iu k \, d_\rho H \right) u = 0.
\ee
The compactification and the time transformation (or equivalently, the rescaling) must be chosen to ensure the regularity of the effective wavenumber, $k^2 (1-H^2)/G$, at the domain boundary where $H=1$ and $G=0$. This delicate interplay between compactification and time transformation is best demonstrated with concrete examples.

\section{Examples}\label{sec:examples}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
In this section, we list five examples of compactification to demonstrate the interplay of time transformation and spatial compactification. We map the unbounded domain $r\in[0,\infty)$ to the compact domain $\rho\in[0,1]$, except for Example 3 where the compact domain is $\rho\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]$. A suitably chosen time transformation regularizes the outgoing plane wave solution, $u_{s+}(\rho)$. 

The Examples 3, 4, and 5 are characteristic-preserving in the sense that the coordinate expression of the outgoing plane wave is kept invariant under null infinity compactification \cite{ZENGINOGLU20112286, bernuzzi2011binary, zenginouglu2025bridging}. There is a large family of suitable time transformations given a spatial compactification. The purpose of this section is not to analyze their properties but to demonstrate the asymptotic regularity of the proposed compactification procedure.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Example 1 (hyperbolic tangent)}\label{sec:tanh}
We used compactification with the hyperbolic tangent at the beginning of Sec.~\ref{sec:nic1d} for the rescaled plane wave. This compactification leads to algebraically simple expressions used in studies of wave equations and quasinormal modes (\cite{bizon2017global, bizon2020toy, donninger2020strichartz}). The spatial mapping is
\be\label{eq:ex1_mapping} r = \arctanh \rho, \qquad G(\rho) = \frac{1}{r'(\rho)} = 1-\rho^2. \ee
Choosing $H=\rho$ in \eqref{eq:trafo_helm} gives $(1-H^2)/G=1$ and $d_\rho H=1$. We have
\[ h(r) = \log(\cosh r), \qquad H(\rho) = \rho. \]
The rescaled plane wave is a smooth, regular function given by \eqref{eq:plane_tanh}
\be\label{eq:tanh_plane} u_{s+}(\rho) = (1+\rho)^{\iu k}.\ee
The transformed Helmholtz equation takes the relatively simple form
\be\label{eq:helm_tanh} (1-\rho^2) \, d_\rho^2 u  + 2\iu \rho (\iu + k) \, d_\rho u + k (\iu+k) \, u = 0. \ee
One cannot specify a value for the solution of the differential equation, or for its derivative at the boundary. Instead, one requires that both the solution and its derivative are bounded. The boundary condition is therefore behavioral in the terminology of Boyd \cite{boyd2001chebyshev}. One may then wonder about the status of the Sommerfeld radiation condition. Taking the limit of the transformed equation \eqref{eq:helm_tanh} towards the boundary, $\rho\to 1^-$, and assuming boundedness for the second derivative, gives a Sommerfeld radiation condition for the transformed variable
\be\label{eq:simple_sommerfeld} d_\rho u - \frac{1}{2} \iu k u = 0. \ee
The condition is not supplied separately by hand but follows from the equation. Admissible solutions automatically satisfy a radiation condition.

\subsection{Example 2 (hyperbolic geometry and the conformal disk model)}\label{sec:disk}
This example serves to demonstrate the relationship of NIC, hyperbolic geometry, and hyperboloidal time surfaces. A representation of hyperbolic geometry is given by the hyperboloid model \cite{thurston1979geometry, loustau2020hyperbolic}.  The hyperboloid is the set of equidistant points to the origin in Minkowski space. Below, we demonstrate the calculation in one space dimension, but the construction works in higher dimensions where $r$ is the radial coordinate in spherical coordinates. Therefore, we use the term ``hyperboloid" instead of ``hyperbola" for the surfaces. 

Using coordinates $\{t,r\}$ for the Minkowski space with metric $ds^2=-dt^2+dr^2$, the hyperboloid can be given by $t^2 - r^2 = 1$.
The higher-dimensional generalization of this surface is a homogeneous space of constant negative curvature with a positive definite metric. We construct a foliation of spacetime into space and time by shifting a hyperboloid along time, $(t-\tau)^2 - r^2 = 1$, and use the shift as the new time coordinate \cite{smarr_kinematical_1978, gowdy_wave_1981}, which gives $\tau = t \pm \sqrt{1+r^2}$. Choosing the minus sign ensures that the time surfaces approach the \emph{outgoing} characteristics in our convention for the height function. We get for the height and boost functions
\be \label{eq:ex2_height} h(r) = \sqrt{1+r^2}, \qquad H(r) = \frac{r}{\sqrt{1+r^2}}.\ee
The hyperboloid model of hyperbolic geometry is an open space. Compactification gives the conformal disk model (also known as the Poincar\'e model) of hyperbolic geometry via
\be\label{eq:ex2_mapping} r =  \frac{2\rho}{1-\rho^2}, \qquad G(\rho) = \frac{(1-\rho^2)^2}{2(1+\rho^2)}. \ee
The choices \eqref{eq:ex2_height} and \eqref{eq:ex2_mapping} have been successfully used in time domain computations of wave equations \cite{FodorRacz04, fodor2008numerical, Bizo__2009, ZenginogluKidder10}. The rescaled plane wave in frequency domain becomes 
\[ u_{s+}(\rho) = \exp\left(-\iu k \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}\right). \]
The transformed Helmholtz equation reads
\begin{align*} 
(1-\rho^2)^2 (1+\rho^2)^2 d_\rho^2 u &+ 2\rho (-3+2\rho^2+\rho^4 + 4\iu k (1+\rho^2) ) \,d_\rho u + \\
& \left( 4 k (\iu(1-\rho^2)+k(1+\rho^2))\right) u=0. 
\end{align*}
This equation is algebraically more complicated than \eqref{eq:helm_tanh} but it has similar general properties to the Helmholtz operator in hyperbolic space \cite{stoll2016harmonic}. Solutions have a finite number of oscillations and evaluation at the outer boundary gives a radiation condition \eqref{eq:simple_sommerfeld}.

\subsection{Example 3 (tangent)}\label{sec:tan}
The conformal compactification procedure by Penrose \cite{Penrose, Penrose65} that underlies null infinity compactification is typically demonstrated using the tangent function (see \cite{penrose2011republication}). The spatial compactification is given by
\be\label{eq:ex3_mapping} r = \tan \rho, \qquad G(\rho) = \cos^2\rho. \ee
The compactified domain is $\rho\in[0,\pi/2]$. In previous examples, the choice of the height function was motivated by algebraic simplicity (Example 1) or geometric considerations (Example 2). In this example, we make a choice based on outgoing characteristic speeds \cite{ZENGINOGLU20112286, bernuzzi2011binary, zenginouglu2025bridging}. We \emph{require} the characteristic-preserving property,
\be\label{eq:require} \tau - \rho = t - r\,,\ee
which gives
\[ h(r) = r - \arctan r, \quad H(\rho) = \sin^2\rho. \]
Generally, given a compactification, $r(\rho)$, with its inverse, $\rho(r)$, the height function
\be\label{eq:invariant} 
h(r) = r - \rho(r),
\ee 
preserves the coordinate form of outgoing plane waves under these transformations so that $u_{s+}(\rho) = e^{\iu k \rho}$. The rescaled equation becomes
\be\label{eq:transformed} d_\rho(\cos^2 \rho \ d_\rho u ) + 2\iu k \sin^2\rho \ d_\rho u + \left(k^2 (1+\sin^2\rho)+\iu k \sin(2\rho) \right) u = 0. \ee
Taking the limit of the equation at the outer boundary, $\rho=\frac{\pi}{2}$, gives the radiation condition
\[ d_\rho u - \iu k u = 0. \]

This example suggests that we may adapt the time coordinate to the problem. The requirement \eqref{eq:require} ensures that outgoing speeds are the same in the compactified coordinate $\rho$ as in the original coordinate $r$. Generalizing this procedure, we may prescribe a characteristic propagation profile with a function $c(\cdot)$ setting $\tau - c(\rho) = t - r$. In particular, increasing the outgoing speed reduces the number of oscillations on a spatial grid. 
% We will exploit this freedom to adapt the time transformation to high-frequency wave propagation in Sec.~\ref{sec:numerical}.

\subsection{Example 4 (exponential)}\label{sec:exp}
We discuss the next two examples for historical reasons. These examples appear in Grosch and Orszag \cite{GroschOrszag77} where they point out that spatial mapping alone is ineffective because waves can no longer be resolved on the mapped grid close to the grid boundary. However, by construction of characteristic-preserving compactification, the solutions take the form $u_{s+}(\rho) = e^{\iu k \rho}$. 

The exponential map is
\be\label{eq:ex4_mapping} r = -\ln(1-\rho), \qquad G(\rho) = 1-\rho. \ee
We have great flexibility in chosing the height function. The main requirement is that the term $(1-H^2)/G$ is regular when $G=0$. We demonstrate the case where the outgoing plane wave has the same coordinate representation in compactified coordinates as in original coordinates via \eqref{eq:invariant}. The height and boost functions are
\[ h(r) = r - 1 + e^{-r}, \qquad H(\rho) = \rho. \]
The Helmholtz equation reads
\[  (1-\rho) d_\rho^2 u + (2 \iu k \rho - 1) d_\rho u + \left(k^2(1+\rho) + \iu k \right) u = 0. \]

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Example 5 (algebraic)}\label{sec:alg}
The algebraic map in \cite{GroschOrszag77} is
\be\label{eq:ext_mapping} r = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}, \qquad G(\rho) = (1-\rho)^2. \ee
Proceeding as in \eqref{eq:invariant}, we set
\[ h(r) = \frac{r^2}{1+r},\qquad H(\rho) = (2-\rho)\rho.\]
The Helmholtz equation becomes
\[ (1-\rho)^2 d_\rho^2 u + 2 \left( \iu k \rho (2-\rho) - (1-\rho) \right)d_\rho u + \left( k^2 (1+(2-\rho)\rho) + 2 \iu k (1-\rho)  \right) u = 0. \]
The algebraic map is commonly used for computing black hole perturbations with a suitable choice for the height function that takes the spacetime curvature into account \cite{Zenginoglu08s, Zenginoglu09, bizon2010saddle}. We see in all examples that a spatial compactification must be accompanied by a suitable time transformation for regularity of the resulting Helmholtz equation, which was the missing step in \cite{GroschOrszag77}.

\section{Generalizations and applications}\label{sec:nic_general}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
The examples in the previous section are in one spatial dimension demonstrating the basic idea of scaling out the infinite oscillations towards the compactified domain boundary with a time transformation. Below, we present generalizations and applications of this basic idea to higher dimensions, variable coefficients
% , and inverse scattering problems.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Higher dimensions}\label{sec:high_d}
In higher dimensions, the transformations are performed along the outgoing direction towards infinity. In addition to the time transformation and compactification, we also remove the decay of the solution. In short, we \emph{scale out the oscillatory decay in the outgoing direction and compactify}:
\be\label{eq:summary} u(\rho, \omega^{d-1}) = r(\rho)^{\tfrac{d-1}{2}} e^{-\iu k h(\rho)} U(r(\rho), \omega^{d-1})\,.\ee
Here, $\omega^{d-1}$ are angular coordinates on a $d-1$ dimensional sphere, $h(\rho)$ is a suitably chosen height function satisfying asymptotic conditions as in \eqref{eq:h_cond}, and $r(\rho)$ is the compactification of the radial coordinate $r$ as in \eqref{eq:mapping}. In other words, we introduce the far-field pattern as a variable and compactify.

The Helmholtz equation in spherical coordinates reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:helm_sph}
	\partial_r^2 U + \frac{d-1}{r} \partial_r U + \frac{1}{r^2}\triangle_{S^{d-1}} U + k^2 U = F,
\end{equation}
where $\triangle_{S^{d-1}}$ is the Laplace operator on the $d-1$ dimensional sphere $S^{d-1}$, and $F$ is a source term that we assume to be compactly supported for simplicity. NIC consists of the following steps:

\noindent (i) Scale out the asymptotic, radial fall-off: We introduce the rescaled variable $\tilde{U}$ which scales out the fall-off behavior of the unknown near infinity\footnote{The general weight can be an arbitrary function with the given asymptotic behavior.} via $\tilde{U} = r^{\tfrac{d-1}{2}} U$. The rescaling removes the first order derivative in $r$ in exchange for a low-order term that falls off as $r^2$. The Helmholtz equation becomes after division by $r^{\tfrac{1-d}{2}}$,
\[ \partial_r^2 \tilde{U} + \frac{1}{r^2}\triangle_{S^{d-1}} \tilde{U} + \left(k^2 - \frac{(1-d)(3-d)}{4 r^2} \right) \tilde{U} = F r^{\tfrac{d-1}{2}}. \]
The terms that differ from the one-dimensional case fall-off at least as $r^2$. This fall-off is compatible with the regularity of NIC at step (iii).

\noindent (ii) Scale out the asymptotic, radial oscillations: In the second step, we introduce a rescaled variable $u$ which scales out the oscillations near infinity. The Helmholtz equation becomes after division by $e^{\iu k h}$ (compare \eqref{eq:rescaling} and \eqref{eq:resc_helm})
\begin{align*}
	\partial_r^2 u & + 2 \iu k H \partial_r u + \frac{1}{r^2}\triangle_{S^{d-1}} u +                                        \\
	        & \left[k^2 \left(1- H^2\right) - \frac{(1-d)(3-d)}{4 r^2} + \iu k \, d_r H \right] u = F r^{\tfrac{d-1}{2} e^{-\iu k h}},
\end{align*}

\noindent (iii) Map the unbounded domain to a bounded domain: Having removed the asymptotic decay and oscillations from the solution, we map the infinite domain in $r$ to a finite domain in $\rho$ as in the one-dimensional case of Sect.~\ref{sec:nic1d}. The Helmholtz equation in higher dimensions becomes after a division by $G(\rho)$ (compare \eqref{eq:trafo_helm}),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hyp_general}
\begin{split}
G \partial_\rho^2 u &+ \left(d_\rho G + 2 \iu k H \right)\partial_\rho u
  + \frac{1}{G r^2}\triangle_{S^{d-1}} u \\
&\quad + \left[k^2 \frac{1-H^2}{G}
  - \frac{(1-d)(3-d)}{4}\frac{1}{G r^2}
  + \iu k d_\rho H \right] u
  = \frac{F}{G} r^{\tfrac{d-1}{2}} e^{-\iu k h}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
In this equation, $r$ is a function of $\rho$. The regularity of the equation requires a similar condition on the combination of spatial compactification and rescaling as in one dimension (finite $(1-H^2)/G$). In higher dimensions, we also need the compactification to satisfy $G(\rho)r(\rho)^2>0$ at the outer boundary of the domain. This condition rules out exponentially fast compactifications for the standard Helmholtz equation, such as the Examples \ref{sec:tanh} and \ref{sec:exp}.
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Variable coefficients}

Helmholtz equations with variable coefficients arise in various problems such as seismic full waveform inversion or black-hole perturbation theory. The fundamental solution is generally not available in such problems. We can nevertheless apply NIC if certain asymptotic conditions are fulfilled.

Consider the source-free Helmholtz equation with variable coefficients in one dimension,
\[ a(r) \, d_r^2 U + b(r) \, d_r U + c(r) U = 0 \]
We rescale as $U=e^{\iu h(r)} u$ and map infinity to the origin with $x=1/\rho$. We get 
\[ a \rho^2 d_\rho^2 u + \left( 2 a \rho - b - 2 \iu a H \right) d_\rho u + \left[ \frac{c-a H^2}{\rho^2} + \iu H \frac{b}{\rho^2} - \iu H_\rho a \right] u = 0 \]
The equation has regular coefficients at infinity, $\rho=0$, if the coefficients of the Helmholtz equation satisfy the fall-off conditions $b=O(r^{-2})$ and $c-a H^2=O(r^{-2})$.


There are some subtleties regarding the regularity of the variable coefficient case that depend on the original unbounded domain problem which the equation is derived from. Compactification for equations with variable coefficients is particularly relevant for black hole perturbations where the potential in the equation arises from a non-vanishing spacetime curvature extending to infinity \cite{regge1957stability}. As a concrete example, consider the radial Bardeen-Press-Teukolsky equation in Schwarzschild spacetime describing perturbations with spin weight $s$,
\[ d_r^2 U + \left(\frac{2M}{r^2 f}+\frac{2}{r}\right) d_r U + \frac{1}{f^2} \left[k^2- \frac{f}{r^2} \ell (\ell+1) \right] U = 0.\] 
Here, $M$ is the mass of the black hole, $f:=1-2M/r$, and $\ell$ is the angular momentum number of the perturbation. The equation does not satisfy the regularity conditions. In particular, the coefficient of the first order derivative falls off too slowly as $r$. However, this is a three dimensional wave equation reduced to one dimension using a decomposition into spherical harmonics. Therefore, we must scale out the asymptotic decay along the radial dimension. Writing the equation for the rescaled variable $\tilde{U} = r U $, we get
\[ d_r^2 \tilde{U} + \frac{2M}{r^2 f} d_r \tilde{U} + \frac{1}{f^2} \left[k^2- \frac{f}{r^2} \left(\ell (\ell+1)-\frac{2M}{r}\right) \right] \tilde{U} = 0. \]
The fall-off conditions are now satisfied. The choice of the height function must take the curvature of the spacetime into account \cite{ZengFramework, macedo2018hyperboloidal}. Compactification of this equation is useful for various studies of black-hole perturbation theory \cite{Zenginoglu09, jaramillo2021pseudospectrum, macedo2022hyperboloidal}. 

It would be interesting to investigate applications of this transformation to non-relativistic, variable-coefficient problems that describe wave propagation in heterogeneous media as they arise in computations in electromagnetism or seismology.

\subsection{A null infinity layer}\label{sec:nil}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Null infinity compactification exploits an asymptotic symmetry of solutions to Helmholtz equations that satisfy the Sommerfeld radiation condition: solutions become outgoing spherical waves in the asymptotic domain. Near the scatterer, however, waves propagate in all directions. Application of the time transformation for incoming waves may decrease the accuracy of the numerical solution \cite{calabrese2006asymptotically}. Therefore, it may be preferable to use standard coordinates around the scatterer and restrict NIC to an outer layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:annulus}). 

Layer methods are commonly used in solutions of Helmholtz equations in the form of absorbing or damping layers, such as the perfectly matched layer \cite{BERENGER1994185} or the perfect absorbing layer \cite{wang2017perfect, yang2021truly}. In the case of the null infinity layer, the solution is not artificially damped but transported to infinity. The solution at the outer boundary of the layer is finite and gives the far-field pattern. We can recover the global solution on the entire unbounded domain by reversing the transformations. 

In the time domain, the hyperboloidal layer implements this idea \cite{ZENGINOGLU20112286}. In the frequency domain, we refer to it as a null infinity layer (NIL) because one can use hyperboloidal and characteristic coordinates by choosing the height function $h$ accordingly. The main feature of the layer is that its outer boundary is at null infinity.

Consider the setup in Fig.~\ref{fig:annulus} (compare the left panels of Figs.~\ref{fig:airfoil_freespace} and \ref{fig:airfoil_incident}). We have a scatterer with boundary $\Gamma$. We use standard coordinates in the domain that extends from the scatterer to $r=R$. The boundary of the numerical domain is at $\rho=S$ and the null infinity layer has thickness $S-R$, shaded in light blue. The infinite exterior domain is mapped to this layer through the radial compactification  \cite{ZENGINOGLU20112286},
\begin{align}\label{eq:layer}
r(\rho)=\frac{\rho}{\Omega(\rho)} \quad \mathrm{with} \quad \Omega(\rho) = 1 - \frac{(\rho-R)^n}{(S-R)^n}\Theta(\rho-R)\,,
\end{align}
where $\Theta$ is the Heaviside step function and $n$ is an integer power. We recover the algebraic mapping from Example \ref{sec:alg} for $R=0$, $S=1$, and $n=1$. The differentiability of the transformation across the interface at $\rho=R$ depends on $n$. We have
\[ r(R) = R, \qquad d_\rho r(R) = 1, \qquad d_\rho^{n-1} r(R) = 0 \quad \mathrm{for} \ n\geq 3. \] 
For the height function, a suitable choice is to use characteristic-preserving coordinates \eqref{eq:invariant}. The optimal choice of the coordinate transformation in the null infinity layer depends on the problem. For example, \cite{bernuzzi2011binary} uses $n=4$ to get higher derivatives at the layer interface, and \cite{hilditch2018evolution} chooses a smooth transition function instead of the Heaviside function. Another option is to use numerical methods that can handle discontinuities at the transition \cite{vishal2024toward}.

\subsubsection{Relationship to PML and PAL}
The most commonly used absorbing layer in the literature is the perfectly matched layer (PML) \cite{BERENGER1994185} (see \cite{joly2012elementary, halla2019analysis, pled_review_2022} for current overviews). PML is equivalent to a complex coordinate transformation \cite{chew19943d}. It is very flexible and convenient to use in various coordinates \cite{collino1998perfectly}. Recently, an improved version has been proposed using a complex mapping and rescaling called the perfect absorbing layer (PAL) \cite{wang2017perfect, yang2021truly} inspired by ``exact PML'' methods \cite{hugonin2005perfectly, bermudez2008exact}. Both PML and PAL damp outgoing waves propagating through the layer.

To contrast these methods with the null infinity layer, we demonstrate the transformations on the example of a one-dimensional, outgoing single-mode, $U_{s+}(r)=e^{\iu k r }$. We present the calculations for the layer with $r>R$ to avoid carrying Heaviside functions through the expressions. A simple choice of PML reads 
\be\label{eq:pml} r(\rho) = \rho + \iu \sigma (\rho-R), \ee
where $\sigma$ is a free parameter. The domain of the new coordinate $\rho$ is the same as $r$. The PML plane wave solution becomes
\[ U_{\mathrm{PML}}(\rho) = U_p(r(\rho)) = e^{\iu k \rho} e^{- k \sigma (\rho-R)}. \]
The wave solution is damped exponentially in the layer where $\rho>R$. The outer boundary is located at some $\rho=S$ at finite distance to the scatterer. The damping is stronger for thicker layers and larger $\sigma$. Numerical analysis and experimentation provides guidelines for the choices of these parameters.

PAL applies a compactification to the complex coordinate transformation of PML (see (3.24) and (3.25) in \cite{yang2021truly}). The mapping is the same as \eqref{eq:layer} with $n=1$ up to a parameter which we set to one 
\[ r(\rho) = R + T(\rho), \quad \mathrm{with} \quad T(\rho) = \frac{(S-R)(\rho-R)}{S-\rho} = \frac{\rho-R}{\Omega(\rho)}\,. \]
The compactification leads to singular equations. To remove the infinite oscillations at the domain boundary, PAL introduces a similar rescaling as the null infinity layer
\[ U_{\mathrm{PAL}}(\rho) = e^{-\iu k T(\rho)} U_{\mathrm{PML}}(r(\rho)) = e^{\iu k R} e^{-k \sigma T(\rho)}. \]
The PAL solution is infinitely damped near the outer boundary of the layer irrespective of the thickness as $T(\rho)\to\infty$ with $\rho\to S$. This enhanced damping gives improved decay estimates for the PAL solution than for the PML solution. While desirable for constructing a perfect absorbing layer, the artificial damping obstructs the recovery of the far-field solution. A more general variant of this method is presented in \cite{wang2025novel}.

In NIC, we do not artificially dampen the outgoing solution. The essential benefit of NIC is that the far-field pattern is directly available at the outer boundary. We can construct the null infinity solution in the layer to take the same form as the solution in standard coordinates using the characteristic-preserving transformation $r=r(\rho)$ and $h(\rho) = r(\rho) - \rho$ so that
\[ u_{\mathrm{NIL}}(\rho) = e^{-\iu k h(\rho)} U_p(r(\rho)) = e^{\iu k (-r(\rho)+\rho)} e^{\iu k r(\rho)} = e^{\iu k \rho}. \]
We can also recover the PAL solution without the damping by using a slightly different choice for the height function,  $h(\rho) = r(\rho) - R$, which corresponds to null coordinates.  

While the NIL solution looks the same as the standard plane-wave solution, the transformed Helmholtz equation looks different. In particular, the transformed equation does not require boundary data or radiation boundary conditions to ensure uniqueness of solutions.

\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec:numerical}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
In this section, we discuss a few toy cases that demonstrate how the method works in practice. We apply the transformation \eqref{eq:summary} to the Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:helm_sph} and solve the compactified Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:hyp_general} using four different numerical methods: finite differences, Chebyshev collocation, spectral Galerkin, and finite element methods.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Single mode in 1D}\label{sec:oned}
One-dimensional examples are generally not representative of the difficulties related to the outer boundary problem. However, fundamental aspects of NIC are present already in one dimension. Once radial and angular directions are separated, the technique in higher dimensions is similar to the one-dimensional case because outgoing waves in the asymptotic region circularize and their dynamic behavior is essentially one-dimensional.

We consider the following, one-dimensional, unbounded-domain problem 
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:oneD}
	\begin{gathered}
		d_r^2 U + k^2 U = 0, \quad \mathrm{in} \ r\in[r_0,\infty),\\
		U|_{r=r_0} = e^{\iu k r_0},\\
		\lim_{r\to\infty} \left(d_r U - \iu k U\right) = 0,
	\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The solution to the above system is the single mode, $U_s(r) = e^{\iu k r}$. To solve the equation numerically on the unbounded domain, we apply the rescaling \eqref{eq:rescaling} and the mapping \eqref{eq:mapping} to get \eqref{eq:trafo_helm} with the interior Dirichlet boundary condition at the scattering point
\be\label{eq:hyp_1d}
	\begin{gathered}
	d_\rho (G d_\rho u) + 2 \iu k H d_\rho u + \left(k^2 \frac{1-H^2}{G} + \iu k \, d_\rho H \right) u = 0, \quad \mathrm{in} \ \rho \in [\rho_0,S],\\
	u|_{\rho=\rho_0} = e^{\iu k (r_0-h(\rho_0))},
	\end{gathered}
\ee
where $\rho_0 := \rho(r_0)$ and $S$ is the outer boundary of the domain with $G(S)=0$. The radiation condition is omitted because it is automatically satisfied. In the Sturm-Liouville limit-point classification, this endpoint admits at most one square-integrable solution, and thus no boundary condition needs to be imposed. The outer boundary is behavioral rather than essential: the radiation condition is encoded automatically in the asymptotics of the equation. This feature of NIC is particularly appealing from a numerical point of view: nothing needs to be done at the outer boundary. The inner Dirichlet boundary data is set by omitting the boundary point and providing appropriate source terms. We solve the above system using three different numerical methods: finite differences, Chebyshev collocation, and spectral Galerkin. See \SM \ref{app:num} for details on the numerical implementations.

\subsection{Sound-soft scattering at a circle in 2D}

A time-harmonic plane wave of unit amplitude propagating in the $+x$-direction impinges on a sound-soft (Dirichlet) circular obstacle of radius $R_0=1$. The incident wave is given by, 
\[
U_{\mathrm{inc}}(r,\theta)=e^{\mathrm{i}k x}=e^{\mathrm{i}k r\cos\theta},
\]
A plane wave violates the two-dimensional Sommerfeld radiation condition because it does not decay at infinity; the scattered field, however, must satisfy the outgoing-wave condition. For a sound-soft scatterer the total field vanishes on the boundary $\Gamma=\{r=R_0\}$: $ U_{\mathrm{tot}}|_{r=R_0}=U_{\mathrm{inc}}+U_{\mathrm{scat}}=0$.
Hence the scattered field obeys the Dirichlet condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dirichlet}
   U_{\mathrm{scat}}(R_0,\theta)=-U_{\mathrm{inc}}(R_0,\theta)
   =-e^{\mathrm{i}k R_0\cos\theta}.
\end{equation}
The solution to this problem is known explicitly in terms of a series expansion. Imposing the Sommerfeld condition suggests the expansion (see, for example, \cite{britt2010compact, yang2021truly})
\[
U_{\mathrm{scat}}(r,\theta)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} c_m
   H^{(1)}_{m}(k r)\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\theta},
\]
with Hankel functions $H^{(1)}_{m}$ of the first kind.  We use the Jacobi-Anger identity,
$e^{\mathrm{i}kR_0\cos\theta}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{i}^{\,m}J_m(kR_0)e^{\mathrm{i}m\theta}$, where $J_m$ are the Bessel functions, and enforce~\eqref{eq:dirichlet} to determine the Fourier coefficients.
% \[
% c_m = -\frac{\mathrm{i}^{\,m}J_m(kR_0)}{H_m^{(1)}(kR_0)}.
% \]
The scattered field that is both outgoing and consistent with the sound-soft boundary is therefore,
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:2Dsolution}
U_{\mathrm{scat}}(r,\theta)= \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}
     c_m H_m^{(1)}(k r)\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\theta} \quad \mathrm{with} \quad 
	 c_m = -\frac{\mathrm{i}^{\,m}J_m(kR_0)}{H_m^{(1)}(kR_0)}.
\end{equation}
We obtain the transformed solution by scaling out the oscillatory decay and compactifying (compare \eqref{eq:summary} with $d=2$):
\begin{equation}
	u(\rho,\theta) = \sqrt{r(\rho)} e^{\iu k (-h(\rho)+r(\rho))} \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} c_m \bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}\left(k r(\rho)\right) e^{\iu m \theta},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}$ denotes the exponentially scaled Hankel function defined as $\bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}(z) := e^{-\iu z} H_{m}^{(1)}(z)$. We write the solution in terms of the exponentially scaled Hankel function to ensure the regularity of the terms in the summation at infinity. Improved numerical behavior of exponentially scaled Bessel functions for large argument are exploited in many software libraries \cite{amos1986algorithm}. We can view this exponential scaling geometrically as evaluation along characteristic hypersurfaces of the underlying Minkowski spacetime (see \cite{ZengFramework, minucci2025confluent}). 


\subsubsection{Spectral-Galerkin solution}
Scattering on a circle with NIC is a geometrically simple problem. Both the inner and the outer boundaries are circles. The transformed Helmholtz equation can be most efficiently solved by using a method that takes advantage of this simplicity. We therefore use spectral Galerkin methods to solve the equation with Chebyshev polynomials in the radial direction and Fourier modes in the angular direction. The Galerkin method is particularly well-suited for this problem because it naturally incorporates the periodic boundary conditions in the angular direction and allows for high accuracy with relatively few basis functions. We use the \textsc{shenfun} package by Mikael Mortensen for the numerical implementation \cite{shenfun, mortensen_joss}. The solution $ u(\rho, \theta) $ is expanded as
\[
u(\rho, \theta) \approx \sum_{n=0}^{N} \sum_{m=-M}^{M} \hat{u}_{n,m} T_n(\rho) e^{\iu m \theta},
\]
where $ T_n(\rho) $ are the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, and $ e^{\iu m \theta} $ are the Fourier modes. The coefficients $ \hat{u}_{n,m} $ are determined by solving the transformed Helmholtz equation. \textsc{shenfun} takes care of the correct definition of function spaces based on the boundary conditions. 

\subsubsection{Characteristic-preserving}
Choosing the characteristic-preserving, algebraic compactification of Example 5, Sect.~\ref{sec:alg}, we get for the transformed Helmholtz equation in polar coordinates,
\[ G \partial_\rho^2 u + \left( d_\rho G + 2 \iu k H \right) \partial_\rho u + \frac{1}{\rho^2}\partial_\theta^2 u + \left[k^2 (1-H) + \frac{1}{4 \rho^2} + \iu k d_\rho H \right] u = 0, \]
where $G=(1-\rho)^2$ and $H=(2-\rho)\rho$. This compactification has two nice properties that we used to simplify the above equation: $Gr^2 = \rho^2$ and $1-H=G$. We solve this equation directly in polar coordinates with Dirichlet boundary data given as the scattering circle by,
\[ 
u(\rho_0,\theta)= -\sqrt{R_0} e^{\iu k (R_0(\cos\theta-1)+\rho_0)}, \qquad \rho_0 = \frac{R_0}{1+R_0}.
\]
No boundary conditions are applied in the outer boundary.

	\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth, valign=c]{figs/soft-sound-char-pres-errors.pdf}
	\caption{Left panel: real part of the solution for the sound-soft scattering problem with $k=40$ using characteristic-preserving coordinates. Right panel: spectral convergence of error against the explicit solution for different wave numbers.}
	\label{fig:char_pres}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:char_pres} (left) shows the real part of the computed solution for the sound-soft scattering problem with wave number $k=40$, $N=60$ Chebyshev polynomials in the radial direction, and $2N$ Fourier modes in the angular direction. The outgoing nature of the scattered solution is evident, with no artificial reflection at the compactified boundary. In the right panel, we display the $L^2$ error against the explicit analytic solution for various wave numbers as a function of the number of Chebyshev polynomials. The results demonstrate exponential convergence, confirming the spectral accuracy of the method. This experiment shows that the characteristic-preserving coordinates lead to a numerically efficient formulation for wave propagation problems extending to infinity.


\subsubsection{Characteristic}
The large freedom in the choice of time transformation, $\tau = t - h(\rho)$, allows us to control the degree of asymptotic flattening in the compactified layer. Here we select the characteristic gauge,
\[
h(\rho) = r(\rho)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H = 1,
\]
which yields the slightly simpler transformed Helmholtz equation
\[
G\,\partial_\rho^2 u +\left(d_\rho G + 2\iu k\right)\partial_\rho u
+\frac{1}{\rho^2} \partial_\theta^2 u
+\frac{1}{4\rho^2} u = 0,
\]
with $G=(1-\rho)^2$. As in the previous experiment, Dirichlet data are imposed at the scattering circle,
\[
u(\rho_0,\theta) \;=\; -\sqrt{R_0}\,\exp\left[\iu k\,(R_0\cos\theta-1)\right],
\quad
\rho_0 = \frac{R_0}{1+R_0},
\]
and no boundary condition is applied at the outer compactified boundary.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.44\textwidth, valign=c]{figs/soft-sound-char.png}\hspace{3mm}
	\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth, valign=c]{figs/soft-sound-char-errors.pdf}
	\caption{Left panel: real part of the solution for the sound-soft scattering problem with $k=40$ using characteristic coordinates. Right panel: spectral convergence of error against the explicit solution for different wave numbers.}
	\label{fig:char}
\end{figure}

The numerical discretization employs $N$ Chebyshev polynomials in the radial coordinate and $2N$ Fourier modes in the angular direction, resulting in a fully spectral approximation. Figure~\ref{fig:char} (left) shows the real part of the solution for the sound-soft scattering problem with $k=40$ and $N=60$. The wavefronts propagate smoothly through the compactified domain without spurious reflections at $\rho=1$. In Fig.~\ref{fig:char} (right) we plot the $L^2$-norm of the error against the analytic solution for wave numbers $k=10,20,\dots,60$ as a function of $N$. In each case the error decays exponentially with $N$. This convergence behavior is nearly indistinguishable from that observed in the characteristic-preserving coordinates (Fig.~\ref{fig:char_pres}), demonstrating that the simpler choice $H=1$ does not compromise spectral accuracy. Both gauges yield robust, spectrally-accurate solutions extending to numerical infinity, with the resolution requirement scaling linearly in the wave number.

\subsubsection{Far-field pattern}
A big advantage of NIC is the access to the full, unbounded domain solution. The method not only removes the outer boundary problem. It also delivers the far-field pattern at the outer boundary of the domain. This feature is in contrast to other layer methods such as PML or PAL which need to be coupled to a radiation extraction algorithm. One typically records the fields on a closed surface around the scatterer and uses the free-space Green's function to extrapolate certain current fields to infinity. In NIC, the radiation field can be directly read-off from the outer boundary. The method does not rely on the availability of the free-space Green's function. 

We calculate the far-field pattern for the solution \eqref{eqn:2Dsolution} by considering the asymptotic expansion of the Hankel function for large argument, 
\[
H_m^{(1)}(kr)
  =\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi kr}}\,
    \exp\!\left[i\left(kr-\frac{\pi m}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right]
    +O\!\left((kr)^{-1}\right),
  \qquad kr\gg|m| .
\]
Inserting the asymptotics into the series, we get for the solution \eqref{eqn:2Dsolution},
\[
U_{\mathrm{scat}}(r,\theta) =
  \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi kr}}\,
  e^{i(kr-\pi/4)}
  \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}
      c_m\,e^{-i\pi m/2}\,e^{im\theta}
  +O(r^{-3/2}).
\]
The far-field pattern, $F(\theta)$, is defined as the leading order term in the asymptotic expansion of the solution, 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:farfield-expansion}
U_{\mathrm{scat}}(r,\theta)
  =\frac{e^{ikr}}{\sqrt{r}}\,
   F(\theta)+O(r^{-3/2}),
  \qquad r\to\infty .
\end{equation}
The far-field pattern for the exact solution is then given by,
\[
F(\theta)
  =-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}}\,
    e^{-i\pi/4}
    \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}
      \frac{J_m(kR_0)}{H_m^{(1)}(kR_0)}\,
      e^{im\theta}
\]
In NIC, the variable we solve for is $u = \sqrt{r} e^{-\iu k h} U_{\mathrm{scat}}$. Comparing this with the far-field expansion \eqref{eqn:farfield-expansion}, and considering the asymptotic behavior of the height function as $h\sim r$, we see that the basic idea of NIC is to compute the far-field pattern in compactified coordinates. In NIC, we obtain the far field by simply evaluating the field at the outer boundary. Note that a calculation of the far-field pattern by enlarging the domain size would be very costly because the accuracy of the solution improves only by $1/r$. One would need a very large grid to compute the pattern accurately if no other extrapolation method is used. While this problem can be resolved using the free-space Green's function for the standard Helmholtz equation, it is much harder when the Green's function is not available and requires sophisticated approximation techniques. 

\begin{figure}[tbhp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figs/farfield.pdf}\hspace{3mm}
	\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figs/farfield-errors.pdf}
	\caption{Left panel: Real, imaginary parts and the absolute value of the far-field pattern for $k=40$. Right panel: Convergence toward the far-field pattern with increasing resolution for different wave numbers.}
	\label{fig:farfield}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:farfield} shows the far-field pattern on the left panel. On the right panel, we see that the error in the far-field pattern behaves similar to the error in the numerical solution. In particular, there is no systematic error in addition to the numerical discretization error. This is in contrast to other methods of radiation extraction without the free-space Green's function, where systematic errors due to finite distance extrapolation or approximation must be controlled separately from the discretization error.

\subsection{Sound-soft scattering at an airfoil in 2D}

In the previous subsection we exploited the geometric simplicity of a circular scatterer by using spectral-Galerkin methods. For general scatterers with complex geometry, we turn to the finite element method. Here we consider two-dimensional scattering by a NACA-type airfoil embedded in a null-infinity layer (NIL) using the open-source code \textsc{NGSolve} \cite{schoberl2014c++}.


\subsubsection{Transformed Helmholtz equation in Cartesian form}
We need to write the transformed Helmholtz equation in a form suitable for implementation in a finite element code that uses global Cartesian coordinates, $x^i=(x,y)$.  Applying the hyperboloidal transformation \eqref{eq:summary} to the free-space Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:helm} yields the equivalent divergence form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cartesian-He}
	\partial_i\left(A^{ij}\,\partial_j u\right)+B^j\,\partial_j u+C\,u\;=\;0,
\end{equation}
with coefficient fields,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefs}
\begin{aligned}
A^{ij} &= L \left(\delta^{ij} - \frac{x^i x^j}{\rho^2}\right) + G \frac{x^i x^j}{\rho^2}, 
\quad
B^j
= \left(2 \iu k H - \frac{G}{\rho}\right)\,
		\frac{x^j}{\rho}, \\
C &= k^2 \frac{1 - H^2}{G}
	+\frac{L}{4 \rho^2}
	+\iu k H',
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $L:=\Omega - \rho\, \Omega'$ and $G=\Om^2/L$. See \SM \ref{eqn:derivation} for the full derivation. The choice of the compactification determines the fields $G$ and $H$.

\subsubsection{Null Infinity Layer} In analogy to other layer methods such as PML, we can restrict the relevant transformations to a layer surrounding the scatterer as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:annulus}. We choose the spatial compactification in \eqref{eq:layer} with $n=4$ combined with a characteristic-preserving time transformation, $H=1-G$. The coefficients can be written as
\begin{align*}
A &= \frac{1}{L \rho^2}
\begin{pmatrix}
x^2 \Omega^2 + y^2L^2
 & \left(\Omega^2-L^2\right) x y \\[1ex]
 \left(\Omega^2-L^2\right) x y
 & x^2 L^2 + y^2 \Omega^2
\end{pmatrix}, \qquad 
B = 2 \iu k - \frac{\Omega^2}{L \rho} (1+2\iu k \rho),\\
C &= k^2 \left(2-\frac{\Omega^2}{L}\right) +\frac{L}{4 \rho ^2} -\frac{i k \Omega}{L^2} \left(\frac{2 L (\Omega-L)}{\rho }+\Omega
\Omega'' \rho \right) .
\end{align*}

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figs/airfoil_freespace.png}\hspace{3mm}
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figs/airfoil_freespace_errors.pdf}
	\caption{Left panel: Airfoil with finite element method surrounded by a null infinity layer. Depicted is the free-space solution with $k=40$. Right panel: Convergence toward the free-space solution with increasing resolution for different wave numbers.}
	\label{fig:airfoil_freespace}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Numerical experiments and results}
We choose a NACA-2412 airfoil as the scatterer. We discretize the transformed Helmholtz equation in its weak (variational) form on the triangulated domain using standard $H^1$-conforming fourth-order Lagrange elements allowing complex-valued fields. The finite element space enforces boundary conditions on the scatterer. Spatial resolution of the mesh is controlled by prescribing the maximum grid spacing, $\max h$. We consider two cases: free-space solution, and the incident solution depicted on the left panels of Figs.~\ref{fig:airfoil_freespace} and \ref{fig:airfoil_incident}.

The first case corresponds to the solution of the Helmholtz equation in free space: $H_0^{(1)}(kr)$. Left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:airfoil_freespace} shows the solution for $k=40$. The total domain boundary is at $\rho=1$ with the null infinity layer starting at $\rho=0.8$. We use standard coordinates with $r=\rho$ for $\rho<0.8$. The interface to the layer is denoted by a black circle in the interior (better visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:airfoil_incident}).  We use the explicit solution to verify the accuracy of NIL. On the right panel, we show the error as a function of increasing grid resolution (decreasing maximum grid spacing, $\max h$) for different wave numbers. The solution is as accurate as the grid resolution, not limited by boundary treatment.

Right panel: The far-field pattern at infinity for the NIL solution compared to the field extracted at the interface of the PML solution.}

The second case solves the problem of an incident wave propagating in the positive $x$ direction. Dirichlet data $U|_\Gamma=-e^{\iu k x}$ are imposed on the airfoil boundary. Left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:airfoil_incident}) shows the solution for $k=40$. We see that outgoing waves pass the interface cleanly with no reflections from the outer boundary. A unique advantage of NIL over other layer methods is access to the radiation field. The far-field pattern is obtained directly by evaluation at the outer boundary. On the right panel, we see the scattered field as measured at the interface (orange) and the far-field pattern at infinity (blue). A standard layer method applies a postprocessing step to get the far-field pattern from the scattered field measured around the scatterer. In NIL, the pattern is immediately available through the solution of the finite element problem.

These experiments confirm that the null-infinity layer integrates seamlessly with standard FEM solvers, preserves accuracy for both near- and far-field quantities, and offers a mesh-based alternative to PML for open-domain wave scattering.

% We can solve time-harmonic wave scattering problems on unbounded domains by scaling out the oscillatory decay and compactifying the outgoing direction \eqref{eq:summary}. The relationship of this method to spacetime geometry motivates the term null infinity compactification. The idea builds on Penrose's construction of conformal boundaries in relativity, in which a time transformation is combined with spatial compactification to capture the infinite exterior region in a finite domain. By "flattening" outgoing waves near infinity through a suitable height function, one removes the infinite oscillations that make spatial compactification ineffective. The outer boundary thus corresponds to null infinity, requiring no artificial boundary conditions to enforce radiative behavior.

% A key benefit of this approach is that one can read off the far-field solution directly at the compactified boundary, avoiding any separate post-processing or extrapolation steps. This contrasts with other local approximate methods where radiative fields are computed by an extra post-processing step. Another benefit is that, by adjusting the time transformation, it is possible to address high-frequency scattering problems. Although the present work focused on the constant-coefficient Helmholtz equation, the underlying framework is flexible enough to handle variable-coefficient and even nonlinear wave equations, as long as they admit asymptotically outgoing solutions.

% The examples presented in this paper are simple demonstrations. An immediate next step is to apply this method to more complex scattering geometries in three spatial dimensions for acoustic and electromagnetic fields. There is potential also for applications in inverse scattering and resonance problems.  A rigorous numerical analysis of NIC would also be helpful for applications both in relativistic and non-relativistic problems.
