\begin{abstract}
We solve time-harmonic wave scattering problems on unbounded domains without domain truncation by mapping the unbounded domain to a bounded domain and scaling the oscillatory decay towards infinity. The technique, first developed in numerical relativity for time-domain wave equations, combines ideas from relativity, hyperbolic geometry, and conformal structure. Compactification at infinity allows us to compute the far-field pattern directly on a finite numerical grid while avoiding the outer boundary problem. We design a layer that restricts the transformations to an annular domain. One- and two-dimensional numerical examples demonstrate the method for simple toy problems. The appendices include a detailed discussion of the numerical methods and an explanation of the compactification technique.
\end{abstract}

In this section, we discuss a few toy cases that demonstrate how the method works in practice. Applications to computational scattering problems and numerical studies of complex cases is left for future work.

\subsection{Plane wave in 1D}\label{sec:oned}
One-dimensional examples are generally not representative of the difficulties related to the outer boundary problem. However, fundamental aspects of NIC are present already in one dimension. Once radial and angular directions are separated, the technique in higher dimensions is similar to the one-dimensional case because outgoing waves in the asymptotic region circularize and their dynamic behavior is essentially one-dimensional.

We consider the following, one-dimensional, unbounded-domain problem 
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:oneD}
	\begin{gathered}
		d_r^2 U + k^2 U = 0, \quad \mathrm{in} \ r\in[r_0,\infty),\\
		U|_{r=r_0} = e^{\iu k r_0},\\
		\lim_{r\to\infty} \left(d_r U - \iu k U\right) = 0,
	\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The solution to the above system is the plane wave, $U_p(r) = e^{\iu k r}$. To solve the equation numerically on the unbounded domain, we apply the rescaling \eqref{eq:rescaling} and the mapping \eqref{eq:mapping} to get \eqref{eq:trafo_helm} with the interior boundary condition at the scattering point
\be\label{eq:hyp_1d}
	\begin{gathered}
	d_\rho (G d_\rho u) - 2 \iu k H d_\rho u + \left(k^2 \frac{1-H^2}{G} - \iu k \, d_\rho H \right) u = 0, \quad \mathrm{in} \ \rho \in [\rho_0,S],\\
	u|_{\rho=\rho_0} = e^{\iu k (r_0-h(\rho_0))},
	\end{gathered}
\ee
where $\rho_0 := \rho(r_0)$ and $S$ is the outer boundary of the domain with $G(S)=0$. The radiation condition is ommitted because it is automatically satisfied. The equation has a regular singular point at the grid boundary where $G$ vanishes. Therefore, the outer boundary condition is behavioral, not numerical. This feature of NIC is particularly appealing from a numerical point of view: nothing needs to be done at the outer boundary. The inner Dirichlet boundary data is set by omitting the boundary point and providing appropriate source terms. We solve the above system using three different numerical methods: finite differences, spectral collocation, and spectral Galerkin. See App.~\ref{app:num} for details on the numerical implementation.

\subsection{Scattering of incoming waves in 2D}
This section discusses a scattering problem both in NIC and NIL coordinates. In NIC, the transformation is applied throughout the entire numerical domain. In NIL, the transformation is restricted to a thin outer layer and the domain structure is as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:annulus}.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsubsection{Problem setup}
We consider a plane wave in the $x$-direction, $e^{\iu k x} = e^{\iu k r \cos\theta}$, scattered off a circle of radius $R_0=1$. The incident plane wave does not satisfy the Sommerfeld radiation condition in 2D and therefore is not regular at future null infinity but the scattered solution is outgoing at infinity and therefore regular. The total field is the sum of the impinging field and the scattered field, implying the following Dirichlet boundary condition for the scattered field:
k
For NIC, we choose the characteristic-preserving, algebraic compactification in Example 5, Sect.~\ref{sec:alg}. This compactification has two nice properties: we have $Gr^2 = \rho^2$ and $1+H=G$. We write the transformed equation \eqref{eq:hyp_general} with $d=2$ as,
\[ G \partial_\rho^2 u + \left( d_\rho G - 2 \iu k H \right) \partial_\rho u + \frac{1}{\rho^2}\partial_\theta^2 u + \left[k^2 (1-H) + \frac{1}{4 \rho^2} - \iu k d_\rho H \right] u = 0, \]
where $G=(1-\rho)^2$ and $H=-(2-\rho)\rho$. The inner boundary data reads
\[ u(\rho_0,\theta)= \sqrt{R_0} e^{-\iu k (R_0-\rho_0)} U(R_0,\theta) %= - \sqrt{R_0} e^{\iu k R_0 \left(\cos\theta-\tfrac{R_0}{1+R_0}\right)}\,.
\]


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsubsection{NIL solution}

The transformed solution reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:soft_soln}
	u(\rho,\theta) = \sqrt{r(\rho)} e^{\iu k\rho} \sum_{|m|=0}^{\infty} c_m \bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}\left(k r(\rho)\right) e^{\iu m \theta},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}$ denotes the exponentially scaled Hankel function defined as $\bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}(z) := e^{-\iu z} H_{m}^{(1)}(z)$. Improved numerical behavior of exponentially scaled Bessel functions for large argument are exploited in many software libraries \cite{amos1986algorithm, 2020SciPy-NMeth}. We can view this exponential scaling geometrically as evaluation along characteristic hypersurfaces of the underlying Minkowski spacetime (see \cite{ZengFramework, minucci2025confluent}).


Three representations of the solution in (a) standard, (b) NIC, and (c) NIL coordinates are plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:soft_sound}. The black interior circle on panel (c) marks the interface at $\rho=R$. The solution for $\rho<R$ agrees identically with the standard representation on panel (a).

\section{Discussion and Outlook}

We can solve time-harmonic wave scattering problems on unbounded domains by scaling out the oscillatory decay and compactifying the outgoing direction \eqref{eq:summary}. The relationship of this method to spacetime geometry motivates the term null infinity compactification. The idea builds on Penrose's construction of conformal boundaries in relativity, in which a time transformation is combined with spatial compactification to capture the infinite exterior region in a finite domain. By "flattening" outgoing waves near infinity through a suitable height function, one removes the infinite oscillations that make spatial compactification ineffective. The outer boundary thus corresponds to null infinity, requiring no artificial boundary conditions to enforce radiative behavior.

A key benefit of this approach is that one can read off the far-field solution directly at the compactified boundary, avoiding any separate post-processing or extrapolation steps. This contrasts with other local approximate methods where radiative fields are computed by an extra post-processing step. Another benefit is that, by adjusting the time transformation, it is possible to address high-frequency scattering problems. Although the present work focused on the constant-coefficient Helmholtz equation, the underlying framework is flexible enough to handle variable-coefficient and even nonlinear wave equations, as long as they admit asymptotically outgoing solutions.

The examples presented in this paper are simple demonstrations. There are many potential directions for future research. An immediate next step is to apply this method to more complex scattering geometries in three spatial dimensions for acoustic and electromagnetic fields. There is potential also for applications in inverse scattering and resonance problems.  A rigorous numerical analysis of NIC would also be helpful for applications both in relativistic and non-relativistic problems.

\section{Null infinity and asymptotic flattening}
\label{sec:app_penrose}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The method presented in this paper relies on the relativity literature  \cite{Penrose, Penrose65, gowdy_wave_1981, friedrich1986,zenginouglu2008hyperboloidal, ZengFramework, ansorg2016spectral, macedo2022hyperboloidal}. Studies on compactification in applied mathematics only consider compactification in space \cite{GroschOrszag77, boyd1982optimization, shen2009some}. For wave propagation problems in unbounded domains, coordinate transformations must include the time direction respecting the causal structure of spacetime. In this appendix, we explain the concept of null infinity and clarify the geometric reason behind asymptotic flattening that makes compactification work.  

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Null infinity}
The idea of null infinity as the boundary of spacetime for outgoing waves of massless fields goes back to Penrose, who presented conformal spacetime compactification in a series of papers starting in 1963 \cite{Penrose, Penrose65}. Here, we demonstrate the main ideas in the simplest case of a two dimensional spacetime.

Flat spacetime distances are measured by the Minkowski metric. The Minkowski metric in two dimensions with coordinates $\{t,x\}$ takes the form $\eta  = -dt^2 + dx^2$. The wave operator is the d'Alembert operator for this metric, $\Box_\eta = \eta^{\mu\nu} \partial_\mu \partial_\nu =  - \partial_t^2 + \partial_x^2$. 
This geometric connection between the spacetime metric $\eta$ and its d'Alembert operator $\Box_\eta$ is one indication that spacetime transformations must be considered for wave propagation problems.

Compactifying space on a standard time slice leads to a singular metric that does not allow us to attach a smooth boundary at infinity because space is Euclidean and not conformally compact on a standard time slice.
% Setting $t=\tan \bar{t}$ and $r=\tan \bar{r}$, we get
% \[ \eta = - \frac{d\bar{t}^2}{\cos^4 \bar{t}} + \frac{d\bar{r}^2}{\cos^4 \bar{r}} + r^2 d\sigma^2.  \]
% One cannot capture the singularity of this metric at the boundary $|\bar{t}|=|\bar{x}|=\pi/2$.
We must combine the space and time directions in a suitable way to describe the propagation of waves to infinity. These waves follow characteristics of the wave equation, also known as the null directions of the metric denoted by: $u = t - x$ and $v = t+x$.
The compactified coordinates $U=\tan^{-1} u$ and $V=\tan^{-1} v$ map the infinite interval $(-\infty,\infty)$ to the finite, open interval $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Squeezing infinite distances to a finite range leads to a singular metric at the limiting points
\be \eta = -dt^2 + dx^2 = -du \, dv = -\frac{dU \, dV}{\left(\cos U \, \cos V\right)^2}.\ee
This singular behavior can be captured by the conformal factor $\Omega = \cos U \, \cos V$. The conformally rescaled metric $\bar{\eta}^2 = \Omega^2 \eta$ is regular across the limiting points of the open interval. This conformal regularity is the core feature of Penrose's spacetime compactification method.

The limiting points of the compactified coordinate system correspond to the boundaries of the physical spacetime at infinity. These boundaries, where the compactified coordinates $U,V$ reach their limiting values $\pm \pi/2$, define what is known as null infinity (infinity in null directions). It is often called ``scri,'' after the script ``I'' symbol \(\scri\). Null infinity consists of two separate components: future null infinity, $\scri^+$, where outgoing radiation reaches infinity, and past null infinity, $\scri^+$, where incoming radiation originates.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figs/standard.pdf}
\hspace{7mm}
	\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figs/standard_penrose.pdf}
\caption{A two-dimensional spacetime in standard coordinates $\{t,x\}$ and its compactification in Penrose coordinates $\{T,X\}$. Figure is taken from \cite{zenginouglu2024hyperbolic}.}
\label{fig:penrose}
\end{figure}
	
Reintroducing time and space coordinates, $T=U+V$ and $X=V-U$, we can draw the entire spacetime in a diamond-shaped region $\{(T,X): |T|+|X| \leq \pi \}$ (see Fig.~\ref{fig:penrose}). The conformal boundary in these coordinates is given by \(|T| + |X| = \pi\). Spatial infinity, \(i^0_{\{R,L\}}\), lies at \(\{T=0, X=\pm \pi\}\), where spacelike geodesics end. Timelike infinity, \(i^\pm\), lies at \(\{T=\pm \pi, X=0\}\), where timelike geodesics end. Null infinity, \(\scri^\pm_{\{R,L\}}\), is where null geodesics end. It is the asymptotic region where the conformal factor  vanishes smoothly, ensuring a well-defined metric structure at the boundary, where waves propagate asymptotically. See \cite{Frauendiener2004, kroon2017conformal} for more detail on null infinity as the conformal boundary of spacetimes.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Asymptotic flattening}
A common concern with compactification is how waves are resolved near the boundary of the compactified region. We have seen many explicit examples demonstrating that only a finite number of oscillations exist even though the domain is unbounded. The time transformation, or equivalently, the rescaling in the frequency domain, takes care of the infinite oscillations.

One would still like to intuitively understand this phenomenon that we call asymptotic flattening. We have discussed the impact of time transformation to a propagating spacetime solution in Sect.~\ref{sec:tt} and provided a visualization of the asymptotic flattening in Fig.~\ref{fig:flattening}. Here, armed with an understanding of the global causal structure of spacetime, we discuss more geometric approaches to understand the regularization resulting from null infinity compactification.

The first geometric insight is counterintuitive: From a global causal perspective, standard time coordinates become singular at infinity. This singularity is out-of-sight because it happens at infinity, but the spacetime diagram in the right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:penrose} clearly demonstrates it: standard time slices intersect at the spatial infinities \(i^0_{\{R,L\}}\). Outgoing waves cross the standard time slices for all times, resulting in the singularity of the time-harmonic solutions on an unbounded domain. 

Operationally, defining a standard, time-reflection symmetric time slice requires a two-way synchronization process (coordinating clocks in time), but such synchronization is impossible at infinite distances. Globally regular time coordinates that do not intersect at the conformal boundary circumvent this issue as they break time-reflection symmetry and only require one-way synchronization. For further discussion, see the recent reviews \cite{zenginouglu2024hyperbolic, zenginouglu2025bridging}.

Another way to see that spatial compactification is singular is topological. The Euclidean space, $\mathbb{R}^3$, is not conformally compact. The natural way to compactify $\mathbb{R}^3$ is via the one-point compactification that turns $\mathbb{R}^3$ to the three-dimensional sphere, $S^3$. The boundary at infinity is a single point. Such a single point cannot describe the outward propagation of waves in different directions at different times, which would require a spherical topology at each time step. A genuine conformal boundary of a three-dimensional interior should be two-dimensional. The Euclidean space, $\mathbb{R}^3$, is not the interior of a compact manifold with a two-dimensional boundary.

Contrast this with hyperbolic space, which \textit{is} conformally compact with a non-degenerate, two-dimensional conformal boundary. The hyperboloidal time transformation changes the geometry of time slices to an asymptotically hyperbolic geometry with a smooth conformal boundary. In Penrose's conformal compactification, the topology of future null infinity is $\mathbb{R}\times S^2$. The topology is also indicated in the Penrose diagram Fig.~\ref{fig:penrose}. Spatial infinity is a point whereas future null infinity is a line. In higher dimensions, each point along future null infinity has the topology of a sphere. As waves propagate out, time slices approaching future null infinity can resolve each pulse traveling in different directions and times from the source. This asymptotic regularity is the underlying geometric reason behind the asymptotic flattening visualized for an explicit outgoing wave solution in Fig.~\ref{fig:flattening}.

\section{Numerical methods}\label{app:num}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
We briefly present the numerical methods used in the experiments of Sect.~\ref{sec:numerical}. We demonstrate the discretization in one representative dimension denoted by $x$. The finite difference and spectral collocation methods are implemented using the \textsc{numpy} package for array vectorization \cite{harris2020array} and the \textsc{scipy} package for sparse representation \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}. The spectral-Galerkin method implementation relies on the software package \textsc{shenfun} by Mikael Mortensen \cite{shenfun, mortensen_joss}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Finite Difference Method}
We discretize the solution domain with a uniform grid spacing and approximate derivatives via Taylor expansions. Let $N$ be the number of grid cells and set $x_i=i h$ with the grid spacing $h=1/N$. A function $u(x)$ has values on the grid denoted by $u_i=u(x_i)$. We approximate derivatives by matrix multiplication,
\[ d_x u|_{x=x_i} \approx \sum_j {D_{ij}^{(1,o)}} u_j, \qquad d_x^2 u|_{x=x_i} \approx \sum_j D_{ij}^{(2,o)} u_j, \]
where $o$ denotes the order of accuracy. The coefficients form sparse matrices. Below, we give the spatial differentiation matrices through their action on a grid vector for orders $2$ and $4$. We leave out the $6$th order matrices as they are lengthy and do not demonstrate any conceptual differences.

\subsubsection{Interior stencils}
Second order differentiation matrices with truncation error of $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$ read, 
\[ \sum_j {D_{ij}^{(1,2)}} u_j  = \frac{1}{2h} (u_{i+1}- u_{i-1}), \quad \sum_j {D_{ij}^{(2,2)}} u_j = \frac{1}{h^2} (u_{i+1}- 2 u_i + u_{i-1}). \]
Fourth order differentiation matrices with truncation error of $\mathcal{O}(h^4)$ read
\[ \sum_j {D_{ij}^{(1,4)}} u_j  = \frac{1}{12h} (-u_{i+2} + 8 u_{i+1} - 8 u_{i-1} + u_{i-2}), \]
\[ \sum_j {D_{ij}^{(2,4)}} u_j = \frac{1}{12h^2} (-u_{i+2} +16 u_{i+1} - 30 u_i + 16 u_{i-1} - u_{i-2}). \]
Higher-order derivatives have wider stencils.

\subsubsection{Boundary stencils}
We use one-sided differencing at the endpoints. Below are the coefficients at the right boundary, $x_N$, for stencils of second and fourth order. Second order differentiation matrices have one-sided differencing at the boundary point
\[ \sum_j {D_{Nj}^{(1,2)}} u_j = \frac{1}{2h}(3 u_N - 4 u_{N-1} + u_{N-2}),\]
\[ \sum_j {D_{Nj}^{(2,2)}} u_j = \frac{1}{h^2}(2 u_N - 5 u_{N-1} + 4 u_{N-2} - u_{N-3}).\]
Fourth order differentiation matrices have wider stencils in the interior, therefore one-sided differencing applies at two points near the boundary
\[ \sum_j {D_{Nj}^{(1,4)}} u_j = \frac{1}{12h}(25 u_N - 48 u_{N-1} + 36 u_{N-2} - 16 u_{N-3} + 3 u_{N-4}),\]
\[ \sum_j {D_{N-1, j}^{(1,4)}} u_j = \frac{1}{12h}(3 u_N + 10 u_{N-1} -18 u_{N-2} + 6 u_{N-3} - u_{N-4}), \]
\[ \sum_j {D_{Nj}^{(2,4)}} u_j = \frac{1}{12 h^2}(45 u_N - 154 u_{N-1} + 214 u_{N-2} - 156 u_{N-3} + 61 u_{N-4} - 10 u_{N-5}),\]
\[ \sum_j {D_{N-1,j}^{(2,4)}} u_j = \frac{1}{12 h^2}(10 u_N - 15 u_{N-1} -4 u_{N-2} + 14 u_{N-3} - 6 u_{N-4} + u_{N-5}).\]
For sixth order differentiation matrices, we apply one-sided differencing at three points near the boundary. 

No boundary treatment beyond the one-sided stencils is required at the outer boundary. Including these boundary stencils in the global differentiation matrix makes them slightly denser near the boundaries. However, the method is still very efficient compared to standard boundary treatments. Among the motivations for constructing compact finite difference operators with narrow stencils is the boundary treatment \cite{britt2010compact}. In contrast, NIC allows us to apply standard high-order finite-difference operators using one-sided stencils near the outer boundary. As we discuss in the sections on Chebyshev (App.~\ref{sec:cheb}) and Galerkin (App.~\ref{sec:galerkin}) methods, one can trivially incorporate the boundary behavior into the the function spaces which makes these methods better suitable for NIC.

\subsubsection{Inner boundary treatment}
We impose Dirichlet boundary conditions in the inner boundary. Numerically, the boundary condition is imposed by converting the boundary data to a source term. The discretized Helmholtz equation with coefficients $a(x), b(x), c(x)$ evaluated at the grid points $x_i$ reads
\[ a_i \sum_j D^{(2,o)}_{ij} u_j + b_i \sum_j  D^{(1,o)}_{ij} u_j  + c_i u_i = F_i, \]
where we added a source term $F_i$ which vanishes in the continuum problem. We omit the left boundary grid point, $i=0$, where the Dirichlet boundary data is prescribed. The next grid point uses the boundary data $u_0$ to build the derivatives. For second order operators, we have at $i=1$
\[ \frac{a_1}{h^2} (u_{2}- 2 u_1 + u_0) + \frac{b_1}{2h} (u_{2}- u_{0}) + c_1 u_1 = 0. \]
The boundary data is entered into the source array $F_i$ with 
\[ F_1 = \left(-\frac{a_1}{h^2} + \frac{b_1}{2h} \right) u_0, \qquad F_j=0 \ \textrm{for} \ j>1.  \]
Wider stencils require more non-vanishing points in the source array where the one-sided difference operators must be taken into account. For fourth order operators, we have two non-vanishing source points 
\[ F_1 = \left(-\frac{10 a_1}{12 h^2} + \frac{3 b_1}{12h} \right) u_0, \qquad F_2 = \left(-\frac{a_2}{12 h^2} - \frac{b_2}{12h} \right) u_0, \qquad F_j=0 \ \textrm{for} \ j>2. \]
For sixth-order operators, we have three non-vanishing source points.

\subsection{Chebyshev Collocation Method}\label{sec:cheb}
We follow Trefethen \cite{trefethen2000spectral} for constructing the Chebyshev differentiation matrices. The grid points are the Chebyshev extreme points defined on $x\in[-1,1]$ as 
\[ x_j = \cos \frac{j \pi}{N}. \]
For the differentiation matrix, $D^{(1)}$, we define
\[ 
  c_i = 
    \begin{cases}
      2
    & \text{for $i=0$ or $N$,}
    \\[4pt]
	1
      & \text{for $1\leq i \leq N-1$}.
    \end{cases}
\]
The entries of the differentiation matrix are
\[ D^{(1)}_{00} = \frac{2 N^2+1}{6}= - D^{(1)}_{NN}, \qquad D^{(1)}_{jj} = -\frac{x_j}{2(1-x_j^2)} \quad  \mathrm{for} \ 1 \leq j \leq N-1, \]
and
\[ D^{(1)}_{ij} = \frac{c_i}{c_j} \frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{x_i-x_j}, \qquad \mathrm{for} \ i\ne j. \]
The differentiation matrix for the second derivative is obtained by matrix product, $D^{(2)}=D^{(1)}\cdot D^{(1)}$. 

We change the domain of the Chebyshev extreme points and differentiation matrices from $x\in[-1,1]$ to $\rho\in[\rho_0,\rho_1]$ as follows:
\[ \rho = \frac{1}{2} \left( \rho_0 (1-x) + \rho_1(1+x)\right), \quad D_\rho^{(1)} = \frac{2}{\rho_1-\rho_0} D^{(1)}, \quad D_\rho^{(2)} = \frac{4}{(\rho_1-\rho_0)^2} D^{(2)}.  \]

The Dirichlet boundary condition is provided as a source term by omitting the boundary point and setting 
\[ F_i = - c_i u_0, \quad \text{for $1\leq i\leq N$}.\]
We do not need a treatment for the outer boundary point, $i=N$.

\subsection{Spectral-Galerkin Method} \label{sec:galerkin}
The spectral-Galerkin approach uses global orthogonal basis functions to approximate the solution and enforces the equation in a weak, variational form. We rely on the \textsc{shenfun} package for the implementation of the spectral-Galerkin method \cite{shenfun, mortensen_joss}. \textsc{shenfun} is a Python package that automates this method on simple tensor-product domains. The radial trial functions are chosen from a Chebyshev polynomial space that satisfies the interior Dirichlet boundary condition. In practice, \textsc{shenfun} uses modified basis functions that vanish at the inner boundary, so the solution expansion automatically meets the homogeneous Dirichlet condition at the interior radius. This eliminates the need to impose the interior boundary condition separately, since it is built into the basis functions. In the angular direction, the solution is expanded in Fourier modes, $e^{im\theta}$, for an angular coordinate $\theta$, which naturally account for periodic boundary conditions in those directions. No explicit boundary condition is required at the outer radial boundary. 

\textsc{shenfun}'s interface is analogous to finite element packages. The user defines a trial function and test function on the tensor-product space (radial Chebyshev and angular Fourier). The Galerkin weak form is then formulated by requiring the weighted residual to vanish against all test functions. \textsc{shenfun} provides high-level operations to project the PDE onto the test space and assemble the resulting linear system for the expansion coefficients.

The spectral-Galerkin method follows the same variational principle as the finite element method but uses global trial functions (Chebyshev polynomials, Fourier modes) instead of piecewise local shape functions. This yields highly accurate results with far fewer degrees of freedom for smooth problems, at the cost of much less flexibility in handling complex geometries or arbitrary domains.

\subsection{Single mode in 2D} 
We consider the source free Helmholtz equation in 2D. We set $d=2$ and $F=0$ in \eqref{eq:helm_sph} and write the equation as a sequence of 1-D equations by expanding the unknown in polar coordinates, $U=\sum U_m(r) e^{\iu m \theta}$. Dropping the subscript $m$, we write
\begin{equation}
	 U_{rr} + U_r + \left(k^2 - \frac{m^2}{r^2}\right)U = 0.
\end{equation}
Applying null infinity compactification using \eqref{eq:g} and \eqref{eq:h} gives 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hyp_2d}
	\Omega^2 u_{\rho\rho} - 2 \left(\Omega-\iu k \left(1-\frac{\Omega^2}{K}\right) \right) u_\rho + \left[k^2 \left(\frac{2}{K}-\frac{\Omega^2}{K^2}\right) - \frac{m^2-\tfrac{1}{4}}{\rho^2}  + 2 \iu k \frac{\Omega}{K} \right] u = 0,\\
\end{equation}
Solutions satisfy at the outer boundary the relationship
\[ u_\rho - \iu \left(\frac{k}{K} - \frac{m^2-\tfrac{1}{4}}{2k}\right)u = 0.  \]
This expression suggests that increasing $K$ may not be as effective as in the one dimensional example, especially for large mode numbers. Nevertheless, for high-frequency wave propagation with large $k$, modifying $K$ accordingly should lead to a more efficient solver.

A single mode solution in 2D is given through Hankel functions of the first kind as  $U(r, \theta) = H_m^{(1)}(k r) e^{\iu m \theta}$. After angular decomposition, we get the following expressions for the radial solution in standard and transformed coordinates
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hankel_soln}
	U(r) = H_m^{(1)}(k r), \qquad u(\rho) = \sqrt{r(\rho)} e^{\iu \tfrac{k}{K} \rho} \, \bar{H}_m^{(1)}\left(k r(\rho)\right),
\end{equation}
where $r(\rho)=\rho/\Omega$ and $\bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}$ denotes the exponentially scaled Hankel function defined as $\bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}(z) := e^{-\iu z} H_{m}^{(1)}(z)$. Improved numerical behavior of exponentially scaled Bessel functions for large argument are exploited in many software libraries \cite{amos1986algorithm, 2020SciPy-NMeth}. We can view this exponential scaling geometrically as evaluation along characteristic hypersurfaces of the underlying Minkowski spacetime in line with the time transformation described in step (ii) and the discussion in Sec.~\ref{sec:time} (see also \cite{ZengFramework}).

Many engineering applications require the computation of the far-field pattern. The far-field solution at infinity can be obtained from the asymptotic behavior of the scaled Hankel function. For large $z$, we have \cite{olver1972bessel}
\[ \bar{H}_{m}^{(1)}(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}} e^{-\iu \pi \left(\tfrac{m}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4} \right)} + O\left(\frac{1}{z^{3/2}}\right) \]
The transformed solution \eqref{eq:hankel_soln} evaluated at null infinity reads
\[ u(1) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k}} e^{\iu \left(\tfrac{k}{K}- \pi \left(\tfrac{m}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4} \right) \right)}\,. \]
The far-field pattern is directly accessible at the outer boundary of the domain.

The radial dependence of the transformed solution is plotted for $k=40$ and $m=20$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:twod}. We observe a similar flattening of the oscillations across the domain as in the 1D case. The choice $K=k$ does not remove the oscillations as much as in the 1D case because of the impact of the mode number $m$. The numerical error for the spectral-collocation method is plotted on the right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:twod}.
